\section{Why start}

Most wealth management is paid through fees: a percentage of your assets,
every year, whatever happens. The provider does fine whether you do or not.
Bayesian Capital started from irritation with that model and one conviction:
build something judged \emph{only} on returns, where the person running the
money and the person owning it are the same.

\oneline{The industry is paid on fees; we wanted to be paid on returns.}

\section{The idea: get paid for other people's panic}

Two honest admissions came first. We can't out-research the market on
fundamentals --- thousands of professionals see every earnings report before
we do. And we don't have the technology for high-speed trading or options.
What's left? \textbf{Prices overshoot.} On volatile stocks, ordinary days
include moments of panic where the price dips several percent and bounces
back within hours. If you place a \emph{limit order} --- a standing instruction
to buy only at your chosen low price --- you get paid for being the calm buyer
in someone else's panic. No forecasting, no speed. Just discipline, patience,
and doing it hundreds of times a year.

\oneline{We don't predict stocks; we get paid a small premium, very often,
for buying dips that usually bounce.}

\section{Which stocks}

The strategy needs stocks that are \textbf{big and heavily traded} (so our
orders fill at fair prices) and \textbf{volatile} (so the dips are deep enough
to pay for the round trip). In this market that pointed straight at the AI
complex --- chipmakers, AI infrastructure, power --- with NVIDIA as the
original centrepiece. We knew this concentrated us in one theme; managing
that risk came later, deliberately.

\oneline{We fish where the fish are: liquid, volatile AI names.}

\section{The first model: right idea, wrong speed}

The first system traded \textbf{once a week}: buy a dip in NVIDIA or
Broadcom, sell at a target, repeat. The returns were strong --- but a dozen
trades a year has two fatal problems. You can't compound quickly, and you
can't tell skill from luck on so few trades. The fix wasn't a better formula;
it was a faster clock.

\oneline{The weekly model proved the idea but starved it --- we needed far
more trades.}

\section{The daily machine}

The system that trades today: each stock has \textbf{two models} estimating
what it's currently worth. One is a \emph{learning trend model} (it updates
its estimate of the stock's level and direction a little every day --- the
``Bayesian'' in our name). The other is a \emph{mean-reversion model} (it
assumes the price gets pulled back toward its recent average). Every morning,
each model places a buy order a safety margin \emph{below} its fair-price
estimate --- the margin scales with how wild the stock has been. When a dip
fills the order, we sell at a fixed few-percent profit target, then go again.
A position that goes wrong is sold after 50 days, no debate. Hundreds of
small wins a year, recycled --- \textbf{turnover is the engine}.

\oneline{Two fair-value models per stock, buy orders parked below fair,
quick fixed profits, hundreds of times a year.}

\section{The company: keeping the compounding}

Compounding only works if profits stay in the pot --- and \emph{when} tax is
paid matters as much as how much. Corporation tax isn't taken out of each
trade; it's \textbf{one bill at the end of the tax year}. So every profit we
make in January is still working for us in February, March and December ---
the full amount, compounding trade after trade. At year end the company pays
\textbf{12.5\%} of the year's profit in one go, and the machine starts again.
That timing is a perfect fit for a strategy that trades every day: hundreds of
small wins compound gross all year instead of being clipped one by one. And
the rate itself --- 12.5\% versus roughly half under personal taxes --- decides
over the years whether the snowball grows or stalls. The company isn't
paperwork; it's part of the maths.

\oneline{Profits compound untouched all year; the tax is one 12.5\% bill at
year end --- then we go again.}

\section{The robot: automating the trading day}

Hundreds of trades a year across nine stocks is too much to run by hand ---
and hand-run trading invites typos, missed mornings and second thoughts. So
the daily machine is operated by \textbf{two small Python programs} wrapped
around the workbook. Every morning the first fetches the latest prices and
the account's cash into the workbook, which then computes each stock's buy
order for the day. The second reads those orders and places them with the
broker (Interactive Brokers) as resting limit orders before the market
opens. A human supervises; a machine executes.

This did more than save labour. It made every later ``stand down'' rule
effortless to run: each rule lives as a simple flag inside the workbook, a
gated stock's money is automatically re-spread across the others, and the
ordering program simply finds nothing to send for that stock. When we later
adopted the trend rule and the 9am pre-market rule, nothing about the
execution had to change --- the robot just obeyed the new cells. And
automation protects the strategy's real edge, which was never prediction
but \emph{discipline}: the same orders, placed the same way, every single
day, whatever the mood or the headlines.

If you're asked \emph{``why keep the model in Excel --- why not write the
whole thing in Python?''}, that was a deliberate choice, for three reasons.
First, \textbf{you can watch a spreadsheet think}: every price, buffer and
gate flag is on screen, so before the market opens a human can trace any
order back to its inputs in seconds and see that the day looks sane. A
program holds all of that invisibly --- supervising it means trusting logs.
Second, \textbf{two independent versions catch mistakes that one can't}:
all our research runs on a Python re-implementation of the workbook that is
checked against the live sheets cell by cell, so production and research
constantly cross-check each other. If the live model were Python too, the
checker and the thing being checked would be the same code, and a single
bug could hide in both. Third, \textbf{changes stay small and safe}: a new
rule is one formula in one cell --- you can read it, test it and undo it in
a single edit, with no software release in between and no risk to the
orders already resting at the broker. The programs we did write are
deliberately simple, so when something breaks it breaks \emph{visibly} ---
an empty cell, not a quietly wrong number.

\oneline{One program feeds the spreadsheet, the spreadsheet does the
thinking, one program places the orders --- discipline by machine, and new
rules slot straight in.}

\section{Getting honest}

Growing from two stocks to a book forced the hard question: \emph{which of
our numbers are real?} Three disciplines came out of it.

\textbf{Verified fills.} Our spreadsheet counted a same-day buy-and-sell
whenever the day's low touched our buy price and the high touched our target.
But daily data can't tell you which happened \emph{first}. Minute-by-minute
data proved the spreadsheet was roughly \textbf{three times too optimistic}.
Since then, no trade counts unless the minute data proves the buy happened
before the sell.

\textbf{No re-tuning.} Every attempt to re-optimise the model's parameters on
recent data made things \emph{worse} on data it hadn't seen --- dozens of
tests, same answer. Parameters were frozen. (One genuine formula bug --- the
mean-reversion model was measuring trend instead of noise --- was found and
fixed. Bugs get fixed; parameters don't get chased.)

\textbf{Entry rules for stocks.} A stock joins the book only by passing fixed
gates: clean verifiable data; classification (AI-linked stocks must promise
50\%+ a year, genuinely unrelated ones 30\%+); the model must work on data it
wasn't fitted to; and the stock must make the \emph{book} better, not just
itself. These gates admitted four stocks and rejected ten --- including famous
names like NVIDIA and Tesla, on numbers, not opinions.

\oneline{We measure honestly, never re-tune, and let fixed rules --- not
excitement --- decide what we trade.}

\section{Smarter capital, not smarter models}

With parameters frozen, we spent weeks trying to make the models cleverer:
better volatility forecasts, market-regime detectors, adaptive profit
targets, stop-losses, and a more sophisticated mean-reversion model
(Schwartz--Smith). \textbf{All ten failed our tests} --- each one damaged the
simple harvest it tried to improve. (The appendix walks through all ten,
one by one, with what each taught us.)

What worked was different: moving \emph{capital} more intelligently. Our
workbook pools each morning's cash across whichever stocks are free to trade
--- so if a stock is told to stand down, its money isn't idle, it backs the
other orders that same morning. Three ``stand down'' rules passed every test
and are now live:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{The trend rule (200-day average):} don't buy dips in a stock
trading below its own 200-day average price --- that's not a dip, that's a
downtrend. This is our bear-market protection.
\item \textbf{The record-high rule:} never place an order whose profit target
would need the stock to hit a new all-time high to get out.
\item \textbf{The pre-market rule:} at 9am, cancel any order more than 4\%
below where the stock is already trading pre-market --- those orders almost
never fill, and the rare fills are bad news. Worth about ten percentage
points a year to the book.
\end{itemize}

The lesson, learned ten times: \textbf{changes that give the model new
information and simply cancel bad orders can win; changes that second-guess
how the model trades always lose.} And a cancelled order is never re-priced.

\oneline{Ten clever model upgrades failed; three simple `skip this order'
rules, backed by pooled capital, are what actually worked.}

\section{The stress test}

Everything above was measured in a bull market. So we obtained minute-level
data for 2021--2023 and replayed today's exact book through the
\textbf{2022 bear market} --- a year our AI names fell 40--80\%. Unprotected,
the book lost about 20\% (painful, survivable). With the trend rule active it
lost about 1\% --- because the money that stopped chasing falling AI stocks
was automatically redeployed into the ``boring'' stocks (GM, Valero, CF),
which had a great year. The diversifiers, admitted for exactly this scenario,
earned their place.

One more honesty upgrade closed the loop: our sell orders are limit orders,
so when a stock jumps \emph{over} our target overnight, we get filled at the
better opening price. The model wasn't counting that --- reality pays about
20 percentage points a year more at book level than our cautious models
claimed.

On the careful planning basis the book targets \textbf{roughly 74\% a year}.
That number is built in three layers, and only the first is a forecast:
cautious per-stock expectations (53\%), plus what our real executions
measurably add (sell orders that gap over the target fill at the better
opening price --- 66\%), plus what running \emph{one shared pot} measurably
adds over nine separate pots (idle money gets re-used every morning; we
credit the weakest half of that measurement --- 74\%). The fuller simulation
of the live setup measures around 88\%; we treat that as the ceiling
reference, not the target.

\oneline{We replayed 2022: unprotected minus twenty, with our rules roughly
flat --- and our real-world executions are better than our models assumed.}

\section{If you're asked\ldots}

\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=bcnavy!5, colframe=bcnavy, boxrule=0.8pt,
  title={\bfseries Five questions you should be ready for}, coltitle=white]
\textbf{``What's your edge?''} --- Patience and turnover, not prediction. We
supply liquidity into short-lived panics in volatile stocks and collect a
small premium hundreds of times a year. Our process edge is honesty: verified
fills, frozen parameters, fixed admission gates.

\textbf{``How do you avoid overfitting?''} --- We fit on the first half of the
data, freeze everything, and only believe the second half. Re-tuning lost in
almost every test we ran, so parameters are frozen and only structural rules
that improve \emph{both} halves independently get adopted. Ten of thirteen
proposals died by that rule.

\textbf{``What happens in a crash?''} --- We replayed our exact book through
2022: minus 20\% unprotected, roughly flat with the 200-day trend rule that
now runs live, because gated capital rotates into the non-AI names. The known
weak spot is a very fast crash, which the trend rule is too slow to see ---
that's a bounded, understood risk.

\textbf{``Why these nine stocks?''} --- They passed fixed gates: liquid,
volatile, verifiable data, model works out of sample, and each one made the
book better. We rejected NVIDIA, Tesla, Broadcom, AMD and others on the same
numbers --- the gates don't care about fame.

\textbf{``Why will it keep working?''} --- Volatility and panic aren't going
away, our stocks are chosen for exactly those traits, and the whole edifice
is re-checkable: every claim reproduces from code and data. And when the
regime changes, our protection was tested on a real bear market, not a
story.
\end{tcolorbox}

\section{Appendix: the ten ideas that didn't make it}

Every idea below sounded sensible --- several are what a professional quant
would suggest first. Each one was tested the same way: tune it on the first
half of our data, freeze it, and only believe what it does on the second
half. All ten failed. Knowing \emph{why} each failed is half of what makes
the three surviving rules trustworthy --- so here they are, in roughly the
order we tried them.

\textbf{1. Re-tuning the dials.} \emph{The idea:} markets change, so
re-optimise the model's settings on recent data. \emph{What happened:}
re-tuned settings beat the frozen ones in 1 test out of 18 on the weekly
model and 6 out of 40 on the daily one --- re-tuning almost always made
things worse on data it hadn't seen. \emph{The lesson:} flexibility in the
rear-view mirror is fragility out the windscreen. Parameters stay frozen;
only structural rules can change.

\textbf{2. A fancier mean-reversion model (Schwartz--Smith).} \emph{The
idea:} replace or supplement our simple mean-reversion model with a more
sophisticated two-factor one. \emph{What happened:} it lost most of its
head-to-head tests, and we discovered all our fair-value models agree about
85\% of the time anyway --- a third opinion that mostly repeats the first
two adds nothing. \emph{The lesson:} more sophistication is not more
information.

\textbf{3. A better volatility forecast.} \emph{The idea:} the model uses
yesterday's price range as its volatility guess; replace it with a genuinely
superior statistical forecast. \emph{What happened:} the forecast really was
more accurate --- and the trading got \emph{worse} in 7 stocks out of 9.
\emph{The lesson:} the model was never using the range as a forecast; it
uses it as a same-day panic gauge. A smoother, ``better'' number calmed the
buffers down exactly when they needed to be wide.

\textbf{4. A market-stress detector.} \emph{The idea:} detect when the
market enters a stressed, high-volatility state and pause buying until it
calms. \emph{What happened:} the stressed days turned out to be our
\emph{best} trades in 8 stocks out of 9 --- pausing them would have gutted
returns. \emph{The lesson:} we are paid for buying panic. Switching off in
panic is switching off the business.

\textbf{5. Flexible profit targets.} \emph{The idea:} on wilder days,
ask for a bigger profit before selling --- there's more room. \emph{What
happened:} the extra room genuinely exists, but waiting for it turned quick
two-day wins into long, risky holds; in the honest test it lost money
(one stock's ``improvement'' collapsed from 160\% to 71\%). \emph{The
lesson:} the small fixed profit target is the engine of the whole machine
--- it keeps the money moving.

\textbf{6. Stop-losses on each stock.} \emph{The idea:} sell anything down
20\% rather than wait for the 50-day exit. \emph{What happened:} in the
bull market it cost double-digit points a year, because most trades that
touch $-20\%$ recover; in the 2022 bear it saved almost nothing, because
the freed cash just bought the next falling stock the following morning.
\emph{The lesson:} a stop-loss changes when you book a loss, not whether
your capital walks back into the decline.

\textbf{7. The book-wide circuit breaker.} \emph{The idea:} if the whole
book falls 15\%, halve the size of every new trade until it recovers.
\emph{What happened:} in the 2022 replay it saved less than 4 points on its
own --- and combined with our trend rule it made things \emph{worse},
because it halved exactly the boring-stock trades that were saving the
year. \emph{The lesson:} right alarm, wrong response. (It stays on the
shelf for one scenario: a crash too fast for the trend rule to see.)

\textbf{8. Skipping ``hopeless'' orders using yesterday's close.} \emph{The
idea:} an order 6\%+ below yesterday's close will almost never fill, so
cancel it and use the money elsewhere. \emph{What happened:} those orders
fill about one day in five, and when they do they're our \emph{best}
trades. \emph{The lesson:} yesterday's close is information the model
already has. The same idea re-built on the \emph{pre-market price} --- which
the model can't see --- became the 9am rule that passed every test. One
ingredient changed: new information.

\textbf{9. A second allocation round at lunchtime.} \emph{The idea:} by
1pm, lots of orders have either filled or clearly won't; re-pool the idle
cash and redeploy it for the afternoon. \emph{What happened:} the idle cash
is real, but redeploying it lost the honest test --- because by 1pm the only
orders still cheap enough to fill belong to stocks falling \emph{that
afternoon}. The top-up systematically funded the day's losers. \emph{The
lesson:} idle cash earning overnight interest beats chasing the afternoon's
faders. Routing decisions belong to the morning.

\textbf{10. Weighting capital by fill probability.} \emph{The idea:} instead
of the 9am rule's yes/no, give more money to orders likely to fill and less
to long shots. \emph{What happened:} the probabilities were measurably real,
and every version of the weighting still lost. \emph{The lesson:} a long
shot only costs one day of idle cash, while tilting money toward the
likely fills crowds it into the shallowest, least profitable dips. The
simple yes/no veto already captures everything the probabilities know.

\oneline{Ten sensible ideas, one honest test each, ten funerals --- and
that's exactly why we trust the three rules that survived.}
