\section{The idea, and why it deserved a proper test}

Every morning sits a manual window between the model's calculations and the
orders going in, and some orders are visibly dead on arrival: the model's buy
price sits far below where the stock is actually trading pre-market ---
information the model, which sees only completed daily bars, does not have. The
proposal: exclude those sleeves so their capital spreads across orders that can
actually fill.

Ten structural overlays had been tested against the deployed book and rejected
--- refits, HAR volatility, the regime gate, vol-scaled premiums, per-stock
stops, the circuit breaker as bear armour, and others. This one is different in
kind: it does not reshape how the model harvests; it \textbf{adds information
the model lacks}. That distinction turns out to be exactly why it is the first
overlay to survive.

All results below use the standard basis: verified same-day fills from the
5-minute bars, deployed parameters untouched, the pooled nine-name book (the
loop the workbook actually trades), everything split at 23~May~2025 with
selections made on the train half and verdicts read from the frozen tested
half.

\section{First attempt: depth against yesterday's close --- rejected}

Without pre-market data, ``the bid is too deep'' can only be measured against
the previous close. On that measuring stick the intuition fails twice over.
Bids 6--10\% below the close fill about \textbf{one day in five} --- not
``almost never'' --- and those fills are the book's \emph{better} trades
(median $+2.7$ to $+2.9\%$ per fill against $+2.2\%$ for shallow bids): the
model earns precisely by being on the bid through panic days. Excluding them
costs money: the train-picked threshold lost the tested half ($110.7\%
\rightarrow 105.3\%$). The idea looked dead.

\section{Second attempt: depth against the pre-market price --- the picture inverts}

Pre-market 5-minute bars (04:00--09:25) were obtained for all nine names. Depth
re-measured against the last pre-market print --- the true distance to the
market that morning --- tells the opposite story:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrrrr}
\toprule
bid depth vs pre-market & sleeve-days & fill rate & avg ret / fill & median \\
\midrule
0 to $-2\%$      & 3{,}186 & 63.9\% & $+1.65\%$ & $+2.32\%$ \\
$-2$ to $-4\%$   & 3{,}043 & 23.8\% & $+1.78\%$ & $+2.47\%$ \\
$-4$ to $-6\%$   & 1{,}346 & 13.1\% & $+0.27\%$ & $+2.51\%$ \\
$-6$ to $-8\%$   & 604     & 6.6\%  & \bad{$-0.51\%$} & $+2.63\%$ \\
$-8$ to $-12\%$  & 441     & 4.1\%  & $+0.57\%$ & $+2.68\%$ \\
beyond $-12\%$   & 133     & \bad{0.0\%} & --- & --- \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Bids more than 4\% below the pre-market price rarely fill --- and the rare
fills are no longer premium: a stock that is trading \emph{up} pre-market and
still crashes through a deep bid intraday is a news reversal, not a
harvestable dip (the specific scenario ``bid $>$6\% below close \emph{and}
pre-market up $>$1\%'' filled 3.2\% of the time at $-2.11\%$ average). The
previous close mismeasured the distance; the pre-market price measures it
correctly. The intuition was right all along --- it was pointed at the wrong
reference price.

\section{The rule, and the result}

\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=bcteal!6, colframe=bcteal, boxrule=0.8pt,
  title={\bfseries The pre-market rule}, coltitle=white]
At the morning check, exclude any sleeve whose buy price sits more than
\textbf{4\% below the last pre-market print}. Its capital pools into the other
sleeves' orders as normal. Exits, targets and stops are untouched. Veto only
--- never re-price (\S6).
\end{tcolorbox}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lcccc}
\toprule
 & full sample & train half & \textbf{tested half (frozen)} & max DD \\
\midrule
baseline            & 75.8\%/yr & 45.4\%/yr & 110.7\%/yr & 29.2\% \\
rule, 09:25 check   & 85.4\% & 53.3\% & \good{122.4\%} & 27.5\% \\
rule, 09:00 check   & \good{87.3\%} & \good{59.5\%} & \good{118.4\%} & \good{25.8\%} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

A \textbf{both-halves win} --- the first structural overlay to clear that bar
in eleven attempts. The checks that ten dead overlays taught us to run:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Threshold is a plateau, not a spike.} Every threshold from 3\% to
12\% beats baseline on the tested half, decaying gracefully; 4\% is the
train half's pick and holds frozen.
\item \textbf{Gains are spread}: seven of nine names contribute. The exception
is RKLB, whose deep fills stay good --- no exemption was taken, because
per-name exemptions chosen after seeing results is selection.
\item \textbf{Volume}: the rule fires on $\sim$24\% of sleeve-days --- a daily
discipline, not an occasional call. Trade counts barely fall (601
$\rightarrow$ 590 buys/yr): the excluded orders mostly weren't filling anyway.
\end{itemize}

\section{Where the profit actually comes from}

Per name, on the held construction (each sleeve keeping its own capital), the
rule is roughly neutral to mildly negative --- RKLB gives up the most (tested
half $159.7\% \rightarrow 109.8\%$), MU and MRVL some. If exclusion merely
cancelled orders, it would be a small net loss. \textbf{The entire gain lives
in the pooling}: an excluded sleeve's capital thickens the other sleeves'
orders the same morning --- orders that are 3--10$\times$ more likely to fill.
The honest statement of the rule is not ``these deep orders are bad trades'';
it is \emph{``this capital is badly parked today --- move it behind orders
that can fill.''}

\tabnote{Implementation consequence: the exclusion must happen BEFORE the
Allocation sheet renormalises the cash across free sleeves. Cancelling orders
after allocations are set gives the per-name columns --- slightly worse than
doing nothing.}

\section{When to check: the cutoff grid}

The 09:25 check is operationally tight for manual updates. The full grid
(thresholds 3.5/4/5\% at each cutoff, tested half shown):

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
cutoff & tested half (range) & best train & note \\
\midrule
09:25 & 118.4--130.7\% & 53.3\% & tightest window \\
09:15 & 113.2--120.1\% & 50.2\% & \emph{not} between 09:00 and 09:25 \\
\textbf{09:00} & 114.3--120.1\% & \good{59.5\%} & best train, best drawdown (25.8\%) \\
08:30 & 108.0--114.2\% & 58.7\% & the edge of the cliff \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Cutoff time is \textbf{not monotone} --- 09:15 tests below both its neighbours
--- which says the cell-to-cell differences inside the 09:00--09:25 band are
noise, not signal. What is robust: all twelve cells from 09:00 onward beat
baseline on both halves and cut the drawdown; 08:30 decays to roughly
baseline (pre-market prices two-plus hours before the open are stale).
\textbf{Operating point: the 09:00 check, 4\% threshold} --- the strongest
train half and drawdown, and a relaxed half-hour for the manual update. If the
check happens at 09:10 or 09:20 on a given morning, essentially nothing is
lost.

\section{The variant that failed: adjust instead of exclude}

The natural alternative: rather than pulling a too-deep order, \emph{raise} it
--- bid at 2\% below the pre-market and keep trading. Tested with honest
execution (a raised limit above the open fills at the open):

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lcccc}
\toprule
 & full & train & tested half & max DD \\
\midrule
baseline                      & 75.8\% & 45.4\% & 110.7\% & 29.2\% \\
\textbf{exclude} (the rule)   & \good{87.3\%} & \good{59.5\%} & \good{118.4\%} & \good{25.8\%} \\
adjust: raise to PM$-$1\%     & 67.5\% & 31.0\% & 111.8\% & 32.7\% \\
adjust: raise to PM$-$2\%     & 70.4\% & 42.0\% & \bad{102.7\%} & 32.2\% \\
adjust: raise to PM$-$3\%     & 77.0\% & 52.1\% & 104.5\% & 29.9\% \\
clamp every bid at PM$-$2\%   & \bad{57.1\%} & \bad{26.9\%} & \bad{92.5\%} & \bad{34.5\%} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Adjustment loses on every metric at every clamp level, with the worst
drawdowns of the study. The mechanics: when the model bids 4\%+ below the
market, it is because volatility is high that morning --- the $k\sigma$ buffer
is demanding a deep discount as compensation for knife risk. Raising the bid
overrides exactly that discipline: it buys at a 2\% discount on the days the
model priced entry at 6--10\%, adding $\sim$130 shallow fills into falling
high-volatility names. Exclusion \emph{respects} the model (``your price is
right but cannot fill today --- stand down''); adjustment \emph{overrides} it
(``fill anyway, at a worse price than you asked for'').

\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=bcgold!8, colframe=bcgold, boxrule=0.8pt,
  title={\bfseries The standing discipline}, coltitle=bcnavy]
The morning intervention may \textbf{veto} an order. It may never
\textbf{amend} one.
\end{tcolorbox}

\section{Why this survived when ten overlays died}

Every rejected overlay tried to make the model smarter about its own
information --- better volatility forecasts, regime awareness, adaptive
premiums, protective stops --- and each one damaged the harvest it was trying
to improve. The pre-market rule (like the one earlier survivor, the MU
earnings pause) does something categorically different: it brings
\textbf{information the model cannot see} --- where the stock is trading
before the open --- and applies it as a conservative veto on capital parking,
touching no bid, no target, no parameter. The lesson for future proposals is a
useful filter: \emph{overlays that reshape the harvest die; overlays that add
information and only veto have a chance.}

\section{Implementation}

Manual, from adoption: at 09:00, compare each order row's buy price to the
pre-market quote; skip any sleeve whose buy sits more than 4\% below it
($\text{buy} < \text{PM} \times 0.96$), before the allocation renormalises.
Structured wiring on request: a flag column in the Allocation machine block
(as the 200dma gate's column G), so a ticked sleeve drops out of the cash
renormalisation automatically. Upgrade path: if Script~1 pulls a pre-market
snapshot from IBKR alongside the OHLC, the flags become formulas, the check
moves to 09:25, and the tested-half edge rises from $+7.7$pp to $+11.7$pp with
no other change.
