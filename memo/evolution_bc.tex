\section{Why we started}

Bayesian Capital did not begin with a view about the market. It began with a
view about the industry. Wealth management, as it is sold to Irish private
clients, is a fee business: the providers are paid on assets and on activity
whether or not the client makes money, and the net-of-fee results reflect
that. We thought there had to be a better way, and that it would have to be
built the other way around, with returns first and the operator's interests
identical to the capital's because they are the same pocket.

That starting point did more work than it might appear to. It is the reason
every decision since has been judged on measured net returns rather than on a
good story, and it is where the research culture described in \S9, which is
hard on its own ideas, originally comes from.

\section{The thesis: trade volatility, not fundamentals}

Before a line of code existed we made two admissions. The first is that the
market will always have a better view of fundamentals than we will; competing
with the sell side and every fund on an earnings call is a game a private
operator loses. The second is that we had neither the infrastructure nor any
appetite for options, FX or high-frequency work. Take away forecasting and
speed, and what is left is volatility itself. Prices overshoot during the
day, and a patient limit order resting below fair value gets paid for
absorbing the overshoot. The edge we are claiming is discipline and turnover,
not information or latency --- liquidity provision at a retail scale.
Everything the firm has built since is a development of that one trade.

\section{Stock targeting: the AI trade}

The thesis needs raw material. A candidate has to be liquid enough that our
limit orders fill without moving the price, and volatile enough that the
premium earned on each round trip clears fees comfortably, in a tape that is
flat to rising, which is where a dip-buyer lives. In 2024 that pointed one
way: the AI complex, with NVDA as the obvious first subject and the
semiconductor and AI-infrastructure names around it. We understood that this
concentrated us in a single theme, and we accepted it as the price of the
volatility we needed. Managing the concentration came later, deliberately ---
it became the work of stages 7 to 9.

\section{The first model: weekly mean reversion}

The first system we ran was a weekly mean-reversion model with fixed
parameters, trading NVDA and AVGO: a bid placed on Monday, clamped below the
recent peak, a premium target, a bounded hold. It made good money and it
taught us two things. The useful lesson was that the trade count was far too
low. A handful of round trips a year cannot compound capital, and it cannot
produce statistics you can trust either, so the clock had to move from weekly
to daily. The subtler lesson only emerged later, when we went back over the
same model: its mean-reversion term never once set the bid (0 of 120 weeks).
The clamp below the peak was doing all the work. We have met that pattern
repeatedly since --- simple mechanisms carrying a model while its more
elaborate terms just decorate it.

\section{Phase II: the daily two-sleeve machine}

The daily redesign is the model that still trades today. Each name runs two
sleeves that estimate fair value in deliberately different ways. One is a
Bayesian Kalman filter, a learning trend-follower that carries a hidden level
and slope and scales its noise terms by the day's trading range. The other is
an OU mean-reverter, an AR(1) pull toward a rolling mean. Both feed the same
execution policy: rest a limit bid a volatility buffer ($k\sigma$) below the
fair-value estimate, exit at a fixed premium over the previous close, and
close anything still held after 50 calendar days. The sleeves disagree
constantly --- about half of their entry days differ --- and that is the
intent, though at the level of a single trade both are doing the same thing,
buying a dip and selling the bounce quickly. What compounds is the turnover:
hundreds of verified fills a year at a few percent each, with the proceeds
going straight back to work.

\section{Incorporation}

Compounding at this cadence is a tax problem before it is anything else, and
the corporate structure deals with it twice over. The first point is timing.
Corporation tax falls due after the year end, so every realised gain
compounds gross for the remainder of the trading year. Hundreds of round
trips recycle their full proceeds, and the tax is settled in a single 12.5\%
payment on the year's profit, after which the machine restarts. For a
strategy whose engine is turnover, moving the tax drag from every trade to
once a year is worth real compounding; it is what makes the daily cadence
viable at all. The second point is the rate. Irish corporation tax at 12.5\%,
against retention of roughly half under personal rates, is over a period of
years the difference between the strategy working and not. Incorporating the
vehicle, with trading badges and execution at Interactive Brokers, made the
structure match the strategy: gains compound untouched through the year, one
payment settles the liability, and the pot stays in the market.

\section{Automation: Python around the workbook}

The daily machine is eighteen sleeves computing, placing and booking orders
every morning. At that cadence, running it by hand is a labour problem and,
worse, a discipline problem. So the Excel workbook is wrapped in two Python
scripts that talk to Interactive Brokers. Script~1 pulls the morning's prices
and the account's cash into the workbook, which drives every sleeve's
calculation; Script~2 reads the resulting order grid and places the day's
resting limit orders at the broker before the open. The operator's job
becomes supervising a machine rather than keying orders.

The design decision that has mattered most is keeping the intelligence and
the plumbing apart. The workbook stays the brain and the scripts stay dumb
pipes: fair values, buffers and the pooled allocation all live in cells that
the scripts merely read. This is what later made the gates so easy to deploy.
A veto is implemented as an allocation flag inside the workbook, the pool
redistributes the gated sleeve's capital across the survivors, and Script~2
simply finds no order to place for that name. Every rule we have adopted
since --- the 200dma gate, the ATH $\varepsilon$-gate, the 9:00 pre-market
rule --- went live without a line of execution code changing. The pre-market
rule asked only that Script~1 also fetch the 9:00 print.

The obvious question is why the brain is not in Python as well. That was a
deliberate choice, for three reasons. First, supervision needs visible state.
Every fair value, buffer, gate flag and allocation is a live cell, so before
the open the operator can trace any order back to its inputs in seconds and
see that the day's state is sane. A Python process holds the same state
invisibly, and supervising it means reading logs and trusting them. Second,
two independent implementations catch what one cannot. All of the research
runs on a Python replica of the workbook, re-verified against the live
sheets cell by cell before every study, so production in Excel and research
in Python are continually cross-checking each other. If the live model were
Python too, the checker and the thing being checked would be the same code,
and a specification error could sit invisible in both. Third, change control
stays small. A new rule deploys as one formula in one named cell, easy to
inspect, easy to diff and reversible in a single edit, with no software
release and no restart risk to the orders already resting at the broker.
That is why each adopted gate was wired in over an afternoon. The other side
of the bargain is that the scripts are kept deliberately simple so that
failure is loud. An empty price cell is impossible to miss; a wrong branch
buried in a large program is not.

Automation also protects the edge itself. The edge is discipline and
turnover (\S3), and a machine that places the same orders in the same way
every morning, whatever the operator's mood and whatever the headlines, is
that discipline made real.

\section{Honesty and gates: the research discipline}

Scaling from two names to a book forced the question every backtest
eventually faces: which of these numbers are real? Three answers now define
how we work.

The first is verified fills. The workbook books a same-day round trip
whenever the day's low touched the bid and the day's high touched the
target, but daily bars cannot tell you which of those happened first.
Checked against five-minute data, the sheet numbers turned out to be roughly
three times too high. Since then a trade only counts if the fill is verified,
with the low provably before the high; anything else is discarded.

The second is that refitting is retired. Re-optimising parameters on recent
data beat the frozen parameters in 1 of 18 weekly folds and 6 of 40 daily
folds, and has lost every fresh comparison since; likelihood tests found the
price process homogeneous, so the searches were fitting noise. The rule we
run by is that structural changes can survive out-of-sample testing and
parameter searches do not. One genuine bug was found and fixed along the way
--- the OU buffer's sigma was measuring the trend rather than the noise, and
correcting it to the AR(1) residuals was worth 7--13pp. That was a
specification error rather than a re-fit, which is exactly why it survived.

The third is the admission gates. Stock selection was codified as G0--G5:
data quality and verifiability; classification by AI-factor beta (above 0.20
puts a name under the 50\% planning gate, genuinely uncorrelated names clear
at 30\%); the frozen half-sample split; the both-halves rule for structural
claims; and the marginal book test, under which a candidate must out-earn or
de-risk the book it joins. The gates admitted GM, VLO, CF and later MRVL.
They declined NVDA, TSLA, AVGO, AMD, SMCI and CEG, and a diversifier round
(FCX, NEM, UAL, LEN) in its entirety. The finding from that round is worth
recording on its own: in this era, the names volatile enough to pay the
premium are AI-correlated, which makes the three diversifier seats we
already hold more valuable rather than less.

\section{Beyond parameters: the pool and the vetoes}

With parameters frozen and the book chosen, what remained to work with was
the routing of capital. The live workbook's allocation loop pools each
morning's cash across whichever sleeves are free to trade, so a sleeve that
stands down does not leave its money idle; the capital thickens every other
order the same morning. That mechanism is where the improvements turned out
to live. Three vetoes were adopted, each adding information the model cannot
see, and each measured on the standard protocol:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{The 200dma gate} (breadth-conditional since August 2026, so it
fires only when at least four of the nine names sit below their lines): no
new bids in a breached name while the gate is armed. In the replayed 2022
bear it turns the book's $-14\%$ into $+2\%$, and in the pooled bull loop it
costs nothing. This is the bear protection.
\item \textbf{The ATH $\varepsilon$-gate}: no order may set up a trade whose
exit would need a print above the all-time high. It costs nothing and
removes a genuine trap.
\item \textbf{The 9:00 pre-market rule}: no order whose buy price sits more
than 4\% below the pre-market print. Orders that deep fill about one day in
twenty, and the fills are poor. This was the first overlay, out of eleven
attempts, to improve both halves of the data; it is worth roughly 10pp of
book-level return.
\end{itemize}

Behind those three stands a much longer list of ideas that did not survive,
and the three owe their credibility to it: a HAR realised-volatility sigma
(a better forecast that made the trading worse), a volatility-regime gate
(the book earns from stress, so pausing in stress gives the edge away),
vol-scaled premiums (the fixed premium is what keeps the turnover high),
per-stock price stops (they cut exactly the trades the bull rescues), the
circuit breaker as bear protection (the right trigger with too weak an
action), a midday re-pool (afternoon capital can only chase that day's
fallers), fill-probability weighting (equal weights held up better), and
Schwartz--Smith as a third sleeve or OU replacement (all our fair-value
sleeves correlate at about 0.85, so a third estimator dilutes rather than
diversifies). Appendix~A goes through all ten. What those failures taught
us, ten times over, is that overlays which reshape how the model harvests
lose, while overlays that add information and only veto have a chance ---
and a veto may cancel an order, never re-price it.

\section{The book as it stands}

The standing configuration is nine names, two sleeves each, equal weights,
parameters frozen at admission, with the three vetoes wired into the live
workbook alongside a discretionary carve-out and a manually updated
discretionary log. Two further pieces of evidence complete the picture.

The first is the bear test. Five-minute data for 2021--23 let us replay the
deployed book through a real bear market on verified fills. Unprotected,
2022 costs about $-14\%$, which is survivable and no worse than April 2025.
With the breadth-conditional 200dma gate the same year comes out at $+2\%$,
because the pool rotates the gated AI capital into the diversifiers; GM,
VLO and CF earn their seats in exactly the scenario they were admitted for.
The known blind spot is the fast crash. A name trading far above its 200dma
can fall 40\% before the gate sees anything, which is April 2025's lesson,
and it is the one scenario in which the circuit breaker still has a role.

The second is execution-accurate accounting. The resting sell is a limit
order, so a held position that gaps above its target overnight fills at the
open rather than at the target. 22\% of held exits did exactly that. This is
money the live book has always earned and the research model never counted,
and accounted for correctly it adds roughly a quarter to the
sheet-convention figures at book level. It is a mechanical property of the
machine, and the plan counts it.

On the planning basis, the planned overall book return is about
\textbf{74\%/yr}, built in three tiers, and everything beyond the first tier
is a measured mechanical property of the machine as built. The captive
per-name anchors average 53\%/yr on sheet conventions with out-of-sample
haircuts. The gap-exit execution uplift just described takes that to 66\%.
The pooling uplift, taken at its weaker measured half ($\times$1.12), takes
it to 74\%; one pot beats nine captive slices because idle sleeves' capital
redeploys each morning, and the uplift is counter-cyclical, largest when
returns are thinnest. The pooled live loop itself measures about 88\%/yr
over the full sample (about 125\%/yr on the tested half) at a 24.7\% maximum
drawdown. We treat that as the upper reference rather than the target,
because a full-sample measurement carries the fit's lookahead. The plan line
in the live workbook compounds at 74\%/yr.

\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=bcgold!8, colframe=bcgold, boxrule=0.8pt,
  title={\bfseries The whole argument}, coltitle=bcnavy]
We trade the one thing we can actually be paid for, which is volatility, in
the names that carry it. We run it on the clock that compounds, inside a
structure that lets the compounding keep its gains, and the orders are
placed by a machine so the discipline never slips. We measure it on the only
basis that is real, which is verified fills, and we improve it only where
the evidence survives both halves of the data, which in practice has meant
vetoes rather than re-fits. Finally we tested it against the regime that
has not happened to us yet; that is what the 2022 replay is for. Each stage
of the story above was forced on us by the failure mode of the one before
it.
\end{tcolorbox}

\appendix
\section{Appendix: the ten rejected attempts, one by one}

The three adopted vetoes owe much of their credibility to what was refused.
Every proposal below ran the same protocol as everything else in this
document: verified fills only, any free parameter chosen on the train half
and then frozen, scored on the untouched tested half, and adopted only on a
win in both halves. Ten proposals failed that bar, several of them the
improvements a quant reviewer would suggest first, and each failed for a
specific reason we now understand. In roughly the order we tried them:

\textbf{A1. Parameter re-fitting.} Re-optimise the model's parameters on
recent data as conditions evolve. This is the oldest and most repeated
failure we have: refitting beat frozen parameters in \textbf{1 of 18} weekly
folds and \textbf{6 of 40} daily folds, and has lost every fresh comparison
run since. Likelihood tests find the price process homogeneous, so the
searches were fitting noise. What it comes down to is that in-sample
flexibility is out-of-sample fragility. The rule this failure left behind
--- structural changes can survive, parameter searches do not --- is the
filter every later proposal had to pass through.

\textbf{A2. Schwartz--Smith two-factor sleeve.} A richer mean-reversion
model, short-term deviations around a stochastic long-run level, proposed as
a third sleeve or as a replacement for the OU. It fails walk-forward on the
book, winning 5 of 15 folds, and the broader finding matters more: all of
our fair-value sleeves correlate at about 0.85, so a third estimator is
dilution rather than diversification. It did show something as a Bayes
replacement on TSLA alone --- selectivity rather than forecasting --- but
not enough to re-admit the name.

\textbf{A3. HAR realised-volatility sigma.} Replace the day's range and the
AR(1) residual sigma with a genuinely better volatility forecast built from
the five-minute bars. The forecast really is better, with an out-of-sample
$R^2$ of 0.315 against 0.127 for the lag-1 baseline, and the trading is
worse anyway. As a drop-in, 7 of 9 names lose the tested half (RKLB by
42pp, MRVL by 56pp); refitted, 0 of 9 beat the deployed model. The reason is
that the buffers are co-adapted to the range's spiky behaviour. Yesterday's
shock widens today's buffer exactly after shocks, where a mean-reverting
forecast shrinks back toward normal too quickly. The model is monetising the
range's same-day link to dip depth, not forecast accuracy.

\textbf{A4. Volatility-regime gate.} A hidden-Markov detector for the
stressed regime, pausing or shrinking entries when the probability of stress
is high. The diagnostic killed the premise before the overlay was built:
stressed-regime entries are the better trades in 8 of 9 names (MRVL
$+3.48\%$ against $+1.87\%$ in calm; CF $+3.95\%$ against $+2.37\%$), and
they carry fewer stops. Pausing in stress ruins the tested half; MRVL falls
from 191\% to 77\%. The book's edge is buying panic, and the stressed state
is where that edge gets paid. Switching off in stress is switching off the
business.

\textbf{A5. Vol-scaled take-profit premium.} The buy side is already
volatility-aware through $k\sigma$, so let the exit premium breathe with
entry volatility too. The intuition half-survives, in that high-$\sigma$
entries really do leave two to three times more headroom above the fixed
target, but harvesting it fails. The train half picks $\alpha=0$, the fixed
premium, in 7 of 9 names, and the exceptions are cautionary: RKLB's train
half picked $\alpha=0.75$ for $+32$pp in sample, and the frozen test
collapsed from 160\% to 71\%. What goes wrong is that high-$\sigma$ fills
exit at the fixed premium within 2--6 days, and fattening the target turns
those quick recycles into multi-day holds. The turnover given up costs more
than the extra premium collects.

\textbf{A6. Per-stock price stops.} Exit at $-20\%$ whenever that arrives
before the 50-day time stop. In the bull market the stop costs double-digit
points a year on the AI names, because most trades that touch $-20\%$ are
rescued by day 50 and the stop simply crystallises the troughs. In the
replayed 2022 bear it buys almost nothing, because the proceeds recycle into
another falling name's bid the next morning and get stopped again, a
treadmill; a $-25\%$ stop stacked on the other protections moved the year
from $-10.2\%$ to $-9.9\%$. A stop changes when the loss is booked. It does
not change whether the capital walks back into the decline.

\textbf{A7. The circuit breaker as bear protection.} A book-level trip: if
equity falls 15\% from its peak, halve the size of every new entry until it
recovers. In 2022 it saves only 3.7pp on its own ($-18.2\%$ against
$-21.9\%$ unprotected), because half-sizing neither exits the held losers
nor stops the treadmill. Stacked on the 200dma gate it is actually worse
than the gate alone ($-10.2\%$ against $-2.6\%$), because it halves exactly
the diversifier entries the gate's rotation was winning with. The trigger is
right and the action is too weak. Its one remaining niche is the fast crash
the 200dma cannot see in time --- it fired in April 2025 when the gate had
barely moved --- so it is kept as a concept and not stacked.

\textbf{A8. Deep-bid exclusion against the previous close.} Skip any order
resting 6\%+ below yesterday's close, on the grounds that it will almost
certainly not fill, and pool the capital instead. The diagnostic inverted
the premise: deep bids fill about one day in five, and when they fill they
are the book's better trades, with a median of $+2.7$--$2.9\%$ against
$+2.2\%$ for the shallow ones. The train-picked threshold loses the tested
half, 110.7\% to 105.3\%. The underlying problem is that yesterday's close
is information the model already has. The same idea rebuilt against the
morning's pre-market print, which the model cannot see, became the adopted
9:00 rule. The failure and the success differ in one ingredient only:
whether the veto brings new information.

\textbf{A9. The midday re-pool.} A second allocation session at 13:00:
cancel the orders sitting far below the tape, pool the freed cash together
with the morning's proceeds, and redeploy. The dead capital is real --- 35\%
of the orders that fill have done so before 13:00, and only 3.3\% fill after
--- but no cell of the parameter grid wins both halves. The train pick loses
the test, 117.8\% to 109.9\%, and the cells that do gain on the test push
drawdown out to 32--33\%. The explanation is adverse selection in daily
miniature. At 9:00 the freed capital spreads across the whole book; by 13:00
the only orders still cheap enough to fill belong to the names falling that
afternoon, so the top-up selectively funds the day's losers.

\textbf{A10. Fill-probability-weighted allocation.} The continuous version
of the pre-market rule: weight each morning's allocations by the probability
of filling, given each order's depth versus the 9:00 print. The probability
curve is real and monotone, running from 96.6\% at the money down to 24.3\%
at 3--4\% depth, and every weighting still loses the train half (equal
weights 59.5\% against 49.9--57.1\%). The economics are that a long-shot
order costs only a day of idle cash at interest, while tilting money toward
the near-the-money orders concentrates capital into the shallowest,
lowest-margin fills and gives up diversification. The simple yes/no veto
already captures everything the curve knows.

\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=bcnavy!5, colframe=bcnavy, boxrule=0.8pt,
  title={\bfseries What the failures have in common}, coltitle=white]
Three regularities cover all ten. First, anything that reshapes how the
model harvests --- its sigma, its premium, its exits, its sizing --- loses,
because the deployed mechanism is co-adapted and the ``improvement'' breaks
the adaptation. Second, a true diagnostic does not make a good policy: the
headroom in A5, the dead capital in A9 and the fill curve in A10 are all
real, and all three overlays lost anyway. A diagnostic licenses a test,
never an adoption. Third, the only survivors add information the model
cannot see and act by veto --- and even then the veto may only cancel an
order, never amend it. The variant of the pre-market rule that raised bids
instead of cancelling them lost both halves at every setting we tried. That
record is why the three living rules are trusted.
\end{tcolorbox}
