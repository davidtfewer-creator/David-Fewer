\section{Why start (and why this way)}

The starting observation was about the industry, not the market: wealth
management as offered to Irish private clients is \textbf{fee-driven rather
than return-driven} --- the providers (the Davys of the world) are compensated
on assets and activity, not outcomes, and net-of-fee results reflect it. The
founding conviction was simply that there must be a better way, and that it
had to be built the other way up: returns first, with the operator's interests
identical to the capital's because they are the same pocket.

That framing did real work later: it is why every subsequent decision was
judged on measured net returns rather than narrative, and why the research
culture became adversarial to its own ideas (\S9).

\section{The thesis: trade volatility, not fundamentals}

Two honest admissions shaped the strategy before a line of code existed.
First, \textbf{the market will always have a better view of fundamentals} than
a private operator --- competing with the sell side and every fund on earnings
calls is a losing game. Second, no high-speed infrastructure for options or FX
was available or wanted. What remains when you subtract forecasting and speed
is \textbf{volatility itself}: prices overshoot intraday, and a patient
resting limit order below fair value gets \emph{paid} for absorbing that
overshoot. The edge is discipline and turnover, not information or latency ---
liquidity provision at retail scale. Everything since has been an elaboration
of that one trade.

\section{Stock targeting: the AI trade}

The thesis needs raw material: names \textbf{liquid enough} that limit orders
fill without moving the price, and \textbf{volatile enough} that the premium
harvested per round trip clears fees comfortably, in a tape that is flat to
rising (a dip-buyer's habitat). In 2024 that pointed one place: the AI
complex, with \textbf{NVDA as the first protagonist} and the semis/AI-infra
names around it. The concentration risk was understood and accepted as the
price of volatility --- managing it became stages 7--9's problem.

\section{First model: weekly mean reversion --- right idea, wrong clock}

The first implementation was a \textbf{weekly fixed-parameter mean-reversion
model} on NVDA and AVGO: a Monday bid clamped below the recent peak, a
premium target, a bounded hold. It made strong returns and taught two lessons.
The productive one: \textbf{the trade count was too low} --- a handful of
round trips a year cannot compound capital or produce statistics you can
trust; the clock had to move to daily. The subtler one, learned later on the
same model: its mean-reversion term never actually bound (0 of 120 weeks) ---
the clamp \emph{was} the model. Simple mechanisms doing all the work while
elaborate terms decorate them became a recurring theme.

\section{Phase II: the daily two-sleeve machine}

The daily redesign is the model that still trades. Each name runs \textbf{two
independent sleeves} estimating fair value differently --- a \textbf{Bayesian
Kalman filter} (a learning trend-follower: hidden level and slope, noises
scaled by the day's range) and an \textbf{OU mean-reverter} (an AR(1) pull
toward a rolling mean). Both wrap the same execution policy: rest a limit bid
a volatility buffer ($k\sigma$) below fair, exit at a fixed premium over the
previous close, 50-calendar-day stop. The sleeves disagree constantly (about
half their entry days differ), which is the point --- but at the trade level
both buy dips and sell bounces quickly. \textbf{Turnover is the compounding
engine}: hundreds of verified fills a year at a few percent each, recycled.

\section{Incorporation: structure serving the maths}

Compounding at this cadence is a tax problem before it is anything else, and
the corporate structure solves it twice over. First, \textbf{timing}:
corporation tax is payable after the year end, so every realised gain
compounds \emph{gross} for the rest of the trading year --- hundreds of round
trips recycle their full proceeds, and the tax is a single 12.5\% bullet on
the year's profit, after which the machine restarts. For a strategy whose
engine is turnover, deferring the drag from every-trade to once-a-year is
worth real compounding, and it is precisely what makes daily trading
tax-viable. Second, \textbf{rate}: Irish CT at 12.5\% against roughly half
retention under personal rates --- over years, the difference between the
strategy working and not. Incorporation (with trading badges and IBKR
execution) made the vehicle match the strategy: gains compound untouched all
year, one payment settles the state, the pot stays in the market.

\section{Automation: Python around the workbook}

The daily machine is eighteen sleeves computing, placing and booking orders
every single morning --- at that cadence, manual operation is a labour
problem and, worse, a discipline problem. The solution wraps the Excel
workbook in \textbf{two Python scripts against Interactive Brokers}.
\textbf{Script~1} pulls each morning's prices and the account's cash into
the workbook, driving every sleeve's calculation; \textbf{Script~2} reads
the resulting order grid and places the day's resting limit orders at the
broker before the open. The operator's job shifts from keying orders to
supervising a machine.

The design choice that mattered was keeping the intelligence and the
plumbing apart: \textbf{the workbook stays the brain and the scripts stay
dumb pipes}. Fair values, buffers, the pooled allocation --- all of it lives
in cells the scripts merely read. That is what later made the gates
seamless: a veto is implemented as an allocation flag inside the workbook,
the pool redistributes the gated sleeve's capital across the survivors, and
Script~2 simply finds no order to place for the gated name. Every rule
adopted since --- the 200dma gate, the ATH $\varepsilon$-gate, the 9:00
pre-market rule --- deployed without a line of execution code changing (the
pre-market rule asks only that Script~1 also fetch the 9:00 print).

The obvious challenge --- \emph{why not code the brain in Python too?} ---
has a deliberate answer, in three parts. \textbf{(i) Supervision needs
visible state.} Every fair value, buffer, gate flag and allocation is a live
cell: before the open, the operator can trace any order back to its inputs
in seconds and see at a glance that the day's state is sane. A Python
process holds the same state invisibly --- supervising it means reading logs
and trusting them. \textbf{(ii) Two independent implementations catch what
one cannot.} All research runs on a Python replica of the workbook that is
re-verified against the live sheets, cell for cell, before every study.
Production in Excel and research in Python continuously cross-check each
other; were the live model Python as well, the checker and the thing being
checked would be the same code, and a specification error could sit
invisible in both. \textbf{(iii) Change control is cell-sized.} A new rule
deploys as one formula in one named cell --- inspectable, diffable,
reversible in a single edit, with no software release and no restart risk to
resting orders. That is why each adopted gate was wired in an afternoon.
The corollary is that the scripts stay deliberately dumb so that failure is
loud: an empty price cell is unmissable; a wrong branch buried in a monolith
is not.

Automation also enforces the strategy's actual edge. The edge is discipline
and turnover (\S3); a machine placing identical orders identically every
morning, immune to the operator's mood and the day's headlines, is that
discipline made literal.

\section{Honesty and gates: the research discipline}

Scaling from two names to a book forced the question every backtest faces:
\emph{which of these numbers are real?} Three answers now define the practice.

\textbf{Verified fills.} The workbook books a same-day round trip whenever the
day's low touched the bid and the high touched the target --- daily bars cannot
order those events. Five-minute data proved the sheet numbers were
$\sim$\textbf{3$\times$ overstated}; every figure since is a verified fill
(low provably before high) or it is not counted.

\textbf{Refitting retired.} Parameter re-optimisation beat frozen parameters
in 1 of 18 weekly folds, 6 of 40 daily folds, and lost every fresh-protocol
comparison; likelihood tests found the price process homogeneous. The
standing law: \emph{structural changes can survive; parameter searches do
not.} One genuine bug was fixed along the way (the OU buffer's sigma measured
the trend, not the noise; corrected to AR(1) residuals, worth +7--13pp) ---
the exception that proves the rule, a specification error rather than a fit.

\textbf{Admission gates.} Stock choice was codified as G0--G5: data \&
verifiability; classification by AI-factor beta ($>$0.20 $\Rightarrow$ the
\textbf{50\%} planning gate, uncorrelated names \textbf{30\%}); the frozen
half-sample split; the both-halves rule for structural claims; and the
marginal book test --- a name must out-earn or de-risk the book it joins.
The gates admitted GM, VLO, CF, then MRVL; they declined NVDA, TSLA, AVGO,
AMD, SMCI, CEG, and a diversifier round (FCX, NEM, UAL, LEN) in its entirety
--- the structural finding there being that names volatile enough to pay the
premium are AI-correlated in this era, which raises the value of the three
diversifier seats already held.

\section{Beyond parameters: the pool, the vetoes, and the graveyard}

With parameters frozen and the book chosen, the remaining degrees of freedom
were \emph{capital routing}. The live workbook's allocation loop pools each
morning's cash across the free sleeves --- so a sleeve that stands down does
not idle, it thickens every other order. That mechanism turned out to be where
improvements live. Three vetoes were adopted, each adding information the
model cannot see and each measured on the standard protocol:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{The 200dma gate}: no new bids in a name whose close sits below
its 200-day average. In the replayed 2022 bear it turns $-20\%$ into
$-1.3\%$; in the pooled bull loop it is free. Bear armour.
\item \textbf{The ATH $\varepsilon$-gate}: no order may construct a trade
whose exit needs a print above the all-time high. Free; removes a real trap.
\item \textbf{The 9:00 pre-market rule}: no order whose buy price sits more
than 4\% below the pre-market print --- such orders fill one day in
twenty and fill badly. The first overlay in eleven attempts to improve both
halves: $\sim$10pp of book-level return.
\end{itemize}

Behind those three stands the graveyard that gives them credibility: HAR
realised-volatility sigma (better forecasts, worse trading), a
volatility-regime gate (the book \emph{earns} from stress), vol-scaled
premiums (the fixed premium is a turnover engine), per-stock price stops
(they cut the trades the bull rescues), the circuit breaker as bear armour
(right trigger, weak action), a midday re-pool (afternoon capital can only
chase fallers), fill-probability weighting (equal weights stand), and
Schwartz--Smith as a third sleeve or OU replacement (all fair-value sleeves
correlate $\sim$0.85 --- a third estimator is dilution). Each of the ten is
dissected in Appendix~A. The meta-lesson,
earned ten times: \textbf{overlays that reshape the harvest die; overlays
that add information and only veto have a chance --- and a veto may cancel an
order, never re-price it.}

\section{The book, stress-tested and correctly accounted}

The standing configuration: \textbf{nine names, two sleeves each, equal
weights, parameters frozen at admission}, the three vetoes wired into the
live workbook alongside a discretionary carve-out and a manually-updated
discretionary log. Two final pieces of evidence complete the picture.

\textbf{The bear test.} User-supplied 2021--23 five-minute data allowed the
deployed book to be replayed through a real bear market on verified fills.
Unprotected, 2022 costs $-20\%$ (survivable --- no worse than April 2025);
with the 200dma gate, $-1.3\%$, because the pool rotates gated AI capital
into the diversifiers --- \textbf{GM, VLO and CF earn their seats in exactly
the scenario they were admitted for}. The known blind spot is the fast crash
(a name far above its 200dma can fall 40\% unseen --- April 2025's lesson),
which is the circuit breaker's residual niche.

\textbf{Execution-accurate accounting.} The resting sell is a limit order:
a held position that gaps above its target overnight fills at the open, not
the target. 22\% of held exits did exactly that --- money the live book has
always earned and the research model never counted. Correctly accounted, the
book of that date planned \textbf{$\approx$79\%/yr} (sheet convention 65\%),
with the pooled live loop measuring $\approx$\textbf{106\%/yr} full-sample
($\approx$142\%/yr on the tested half) at unchanged drawdown.

\textbf{Composition change (2 September 2026).} RKLB left the book and AVGO
took its slot --- a risk decision, not a model one: RKLB's post-IPO
performance is partly SpaceX-linked, it carries binary event risk (the
Neutron launch), and its admission was judged in hindsight to have ridden
volatility and excitement ahead of the SpaceX IPO. The back-test cannot price
those risks, so it did not get a veto. The cost is stated plainly: RKLB was
the book's yield engine (planning anchor 156\%/yr against AVGO's 51\%), so
the \textbf{planned overall book return re-casts to $\approx$66\%/yr}
execution-accurate (sheet convention 53\%), with the pooled live loop
measuring $\approx$88\%/yr full-sample ($\approx$125\%/yr tested half). In
exchange the book sheds an unpriced binary exposure, its worst drawdown
improves (25.8\% $\to$ 24.7\% --- RKLB was where the pool concentrated in the
April 2025 stress), and average correlation falls. The plan line in the live
workbook compounds at the new figure from the swap date.

\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=bcgold!8, colframe=bcgold, boxrule=0.8pt,
  title={\bfseries The thought process, compressed}, coltitle=bcnavy]
Trade the thing you can be paid for (volatility), in the names that carry it
(the AI trade), on the clock that compounds (daily), inside the structure
that keeps the compounding (12.5\% CT), by the hand that never wavers
(Python around the workbook, orders resting at IBKR before the open),
measured on the only basis that is real (verified fills), improved only
where evidence survives both halves (vetoes, not re-fits), and stress-tested
against the regime that hasn't happened yet (2022 replay). Each stage was
forced by the failure mode of the one before it.
\end{tcolorbox}

\section{Appendix: the ten rejected attempts, one by one}

The three adopted vetoes owe their credibility to what was refused. Every
proposal below ran the same protocol as everything else in this document:
verified fills only, any free parameter chosen on the train half and frozen,
scored on the untouched tested half, adopted only on a both-halves win. Ten
proposals --- several of them the ``obvious'' improvements a quant reviewer
would suggest first --- failed that bar, each with a specific, understood
mechanism. In roughly the order attempted:

\textbf{A1. Parameter re-fitting.} Re-optimise the model's parameters on
recent data as conditions evolve. The oldest and most repeated failure:
refitting beat frozen parameters in \textbf{1 of 18} weekly folds and
\textbf{6 of 40} daily folds, and lost every fresh-protocol comparison since;
likelihood tests find the price process homogeneous, so the searches were
fitting noise. \emph{Mechanism:} in-sample flexibility is out-of-sample
fragility. The standing law --- structural changes can survive; parameter
searches do not --- is the filter every later proposal passed through.

\textbf{A2. Schwartz--Smith two-factor sleeve.} A richer mean-reversion
model (short-term deviations around a stochastic long-run level) as a third
sleeve or an OU replacement. Fails walk-forward on the book (5 of 15 folds);
and the structural finding is broader: \emph{all} fair-value sleeves
correlate $\sim$0.85, so a third estimator is dilution, not diversification.
(Suggestive as a Bayes \emph{replacement} on TSLA only --- selectivity, not
forecasting --- but not enough to re-admit the name.)

\textbf{A3. HAR realised-volatility sigma.} Replace the day's range and the
AR(1) residual sigma with a genuinely better volatility forecast built from
the five-minute bars. The forecast \emph{is} better (out-of-sample $R^2$
0.315 vs 0.127 for lag-1) --- and the trading is worse: as a drop-in, 7 of 9
names lose the tested half (RKLB $-42$pp, MRVL $-56$pp); refit, 0 of 9 beat
deployed. \emph{Mechanism:} the buffers are co-adapted to the range's spiky
dynamics --- yesterday's shock widens today's buffer exactly after shocks,
where a mean-reverting forecast shrinks back toward normal too fast. The
model monetises the range's contemporaneous link to next-day dip depth, not
forecast accuracy.

\textbf{A4. Volatility-regime gate.} A hidden-Markov detector for the
stressed regime, pausing or shrinking entries when stress probability is
high. The diagnostic killed the premise before the overlay could: stressed-%
regime entries are the \emph{better} trades in 8 of 9 names (MRVL $+3.48\%$
vs $+1.87\%$ calm; CF $+3.95\%$ vs $+2.37\%$) and carry fewer stops. Pausing
butchers the tested half (MRVL 191\%$\to$77\%). \emph{Mechanism:} the book's
edge is buying panic; the stressed state is where it gets paid. Stress is
the product, not the hazard.

\textbf{A5. Vol-scaled take-profit premium.} The buy side is vol-aware
($k\sigma$); let the exit premium breathe with entry volatility too. The
intuition half-survives --- high-$\sigma$ entries really do leave 2--3$\times$
more headroom above the fixed target --- but harvesting it fails: the train
half picks $\alpha=0$ (fixed premium) in 7 of 9 names, and the exceptions are
cautionary tales (RKLB train-picked $\alpha=0.75$ for $+32$pp in sample; the
frozen test collapsed 160\%$\to$71\%). \emph{Mechanism:} high-$\sigma$ fills
at the fixed premium exit in 2--6 days; fattening the target converts quick
recycles into multi-day holds, and the foregone turnover costs more than the
extra premium collects. The fixed premium is a turnover engine.

\textbf{A6. Per-stock price stops.} Exit at $-20\%$ whenever it arrives
before the 50-day time stop. In the bull the stop charges double-digit
points a year on the AI names --- most trades that touch $-20\%$ are rescued
by day 50, so the stop crystallises troughs. In the replayed 2022 bear it
buys almost nothing: the proceeds recycle into another falling name's bid
the next morning and stop again (the \emph{treadmill}); a $-25\%$ stop
stacked on the other protections moved the year from $-10.2\%$ to $-9.9\%$.
\emph{Mechanism:} a stop changes when a loss is booked, not whether the
capital re-enters the decline.

\textbf{A7. The circuit breaker as bear armour.} A book-level trip (equity
$-15\%$ from peak $\Rightarrow$ half-size entries until recovery). In 2022
it saves only 3.7pp ($-18.2\%$ vs $-21.9\%$ unprotected) --- half-sizing
neither exits held losers nor stops the treadmill --- and \emph{stacked on
the 200dma gate it is worse than the gate alone} ($-10.2\%$ vs $-2.6\%$),
because it half-sizes exactly the diversifier entries the gate's rotation
was winning with. \emph{Mechanism:} right trigger, weak action. Its residual
niche is the fast crash the 200dma is too slow to see (it fired in April
2025 when the gate barely moved) --- kept as a concept, not stacked.

\textbf{A8. Deep-bid exclusion against the previous close.} Skip orders
resting 6\%+ below yesterday's close --- ``they almost definitely won't
fill'' --- and pool the capital. The diagnostic inverted the premise: deep
bids fill about one day in five, and their fills are the book's
\emph{better} trades (median $+2.7$--$2.9\%$ vs $+2.2\%$ shallow). The
train-picked threshold loses the tested half (110.7\%$\to$105.3\%).
\emph{Mechanism:} yesterday's close is information the model already has.
The same idea measured against this morning's pre-market print --- which the
model cannot see --- became the adopted 9:00 rule. The failure and the
success differ only in whether the veto adds information.

\textbf{A9. The midday re-pool.} A second allocation session at 13:00:
cancel orders far below the tape, re-pool the freed cash and the morning's
proceeds. The dead capital is real (35\% of filling orders fill before
13:00, 3.3\% after) --- but no grid cell wins both halves: the train pick
loses the test (117.8\%$\to$109.9\%), and the cells that gain on the test
blow drawdown to 32--33\%. \emph{Mechanism:} at 9:00 freed capital spreads
across the whole book; by 13:00 the only orders still fillable belong to
names falling \emph{that afternoon} --- the top-up selectively funds the
day's faders. Adverse selection in daily miniature.

\textbf{A10. Fill-probability-weighted allocation.} The continuous version
of the pre-market rule: weight each morning's allocations by fill
probability given depth versus the 9:00 print. The probability curve is
real and monotone (from 96.6\% at-the-money to 24.3\% at 3--4\% depth) ---
yet every weighting loses the train half (equal 59.5\% vs 49.9--57.1\%).
\emph{Mechanism:} equal-weighting a low-probability order costs one day of
idle cash at interest; weighting toward near-the-money orders concentrates
capital into shallow, low-margin fills and gives up diversification. The
binary veto captures everything the curve has to give.

\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=bcnavy!5, colframe=bcnavy, boxrule=0.8pt,
  title={\bfseries What the graveyard teaches}, coltitle=white]
Three regularities cover all ten. \textbf{(i)} Anything that re-shapes how
the model harvests --- its sigma, its premium, its exits, its sizing ---
loses, because the deployed mechanism is co-adapted and the ``improvement''
breaks the adaptation. \textbf{(ii)} Anything justified by a true diagnostic
can still fail as a policy: the headroom (A5), the dead capital (A9) and the
fill curve (A10) are all real, and all three overlays lost. Diagnostics
license a test, never an adoption. \textbf{(iii)} The only survivors add
information the model cannot see and act by veto --- and even then the veto
may only \emph{cancel} an order, never amend it: the raise-the-bid variant
of the pre-market rule lost both halves at every clamp. Ten funerals are why
the three living rules are trusted.
