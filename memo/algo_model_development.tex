\section{Scope of this note}

Bayesian Capital designed, implemented, tested and deployed a systematic trading system over the
course of 2026. The system runs a live nine-stock equity book from a spreadsheet-and-broker
production environment, with every entry price computed by one of two statistical models built
in-house. This note summarises the mathematical content of that development for readers who do
not need to work through it in detail; the full treatment --- derivations, calibration evidence,
and the out-of-sample validation programme --- is held in the firm's internal research
documentation.

The development comprises four distinct bodies of work: two statistical pricing engines, a
derivative-free global optimisation framework for fitting them, and a validation programme that
determines what is and is not deployed. Each is summarised below.

\section{The strategy and its capital mechanics}

The book harvests short-term volatility: it buys weakness at a model-computed discount and sells
a fixed premium into the subsequent bounce. Both engines emit a single object each trading day
per stock --- a limit bid $B_t$, computed causally from data up to the prior close --- and
everything downstream (fill logic, premium exit, stop-loss, commissions, interest on idle cash)
is shared, so the engines are directly comparable and freely blendable.

Capital is deployed in staggered tranches. For $n$ tranches of $K/n$ each, with $M_j$ the $j$-th
tranche's terminal return multiple over its round trips,
\begin{equation}
\Pi=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{K}{n}\bigl(M_j-1\bigr)=K\bigl(\bar M-1\bigr),
\end{equation}
so book profit is governed by the \emph{average} multiple across start-dates. This identity is
what allows the two engines and nine names to be mixed in one pooled capital loop, and it is why
capital allocation was treated as a first-order research question in its own right
(\S\ref{sec:validation}).

\section{Engine I: a Bayesian local-linear-trend Kalman filter}

The first engine treats a stock's fair value as a hidden state to be inferred, not a formula to
be guessed. It carries a two-dimensional state --- a level $m_t$ and a trend $b_t$ --- observed
through noisy closes:
\begin{equation}
m_t=m_{t-1}+b_{t-1}+\eta^{(m)}_t,\qquad
b_t=b_{t-1}+\eta^{(b)}_t,\qquad
C_t=m_t+\varepsilon_t .
\end{equation}
The modelling decision that distinguishes this from a textbook filter is that all three noise
variances are scaled to the day's trading range $R_t=\bar H_t-\bar L_t$:
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{Var}\bigl(\eta^{(m)}_t\bigr)=(\varphi_L R_t)^2,\qquad
\operatorname{Var}\bigl(\eta^{(b)}_t\bigr)=(\psi R_t)^2,\qquad
\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon_t)=(\lambda R_t)^2,
\end{equation}
so the filter automatically trusts the tape less on turbulent days. The update is the standard
Kalman recursion in precision form --- the posterior mean is a precision-weighted average of
prior and datum, with the gain $K_t=V_t^-/(V_t^-+r_t)$ computed afresh every day rather than
hand-set. The entry bid sits a fitted number of \emph{predictive} standard deviations below the
one-step forecast, floored at the day's open and at a cap below the all-time high $P_t$:
\begin{equation}
\sigma_t=\sqrt{V_t+q_t+r_t},\qquad
B_t^{\text{LLT}}=\min\bigl((\hat m_{t-1}+\hat b_{t-1})-k\,\sigma_{t-1},\;O_t,\;(1-c)P_t\bigr).
\end{equation}
Because $\sigma_t$ widens when the filter is less certain, the discount is a genuine credible
interval that deepens in turbulence --- there are no hand-set weights anywhere in the engine.

\section{Engine II: an Ornstein--Uhlenbeck mean-reversion sleeve}

The second engine was built to be \emph{deliberately different}: where the Kalman filter projects
the trend forward, this one fades deviations from a slow long-run mean, so its mistakes land on
different days. It models price as the mean-reverting diffusion
\begin{equation}
dX_t=\theta(\mu-X_t)\,dt+\sigma\,dW_t,
\end{equation}
which, sampled daily, is an exact AR(1) about the mean, $X_t-\mu=\phi(X_{t-1}-\mu)+\varepsilon_t$
with $\phi=e^{-\theta\Delta t}$. The persistence $\phi$ is estimated each day as the
least-squares AR(1) coefficient over a rolling window of $W$ closes, clamped to $[0,0.99]$ ---
the clamp is substantive, since values outside it assert either phantom anti-persistence or a
non-stationary random walk. The discount scale is the standard deviation of the fitted AR(1)
\emph{residuals},
\begin{equation}
e_s = d_s-\hat\phi_t\,d_{s-1},\qquad
s_t=\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{W-1}\textstyle\sum_s (e_s-\bar e_t)^2},
\end{equation}
rather than of the price levels --- a specification point that mattered: the level-based scale
conflates trend with innovation and was identified, corrected and evidenced as part of this
development (it is the only change to a live signal that has survived the firm's out-of-sample
tests). The bid then takes the same form as Engine I's, a $k$-sigma discount below the one-step
forecast $f_t=\bar C_t+\hat\phi_t(C_{t-1}-\bar C_t)$, floored by the open and the peak cap.

The sleeve's role in the book was itself re-measured during the year: the two engines' returns
correlate $0.57$ unconditionally and $0.90$+ when both hold stock, so the sleeve is carried as a
\emph{timing} diversifier and a return source, not as the near-independent hedge an earlier
analysis suggested. Establishing that --- including showing that a previously reported near-zero
correlation was an artefact of an idle sleeve --- is part of the documented work.

\section{Parameter estimation and optimisation}

Each of the nine names carries a ten-dimensional parameter vector,
\begin{equation}
\theta=(\underbrace{\lambda,\varphi_L,\psi,k,\pi,c}_{\text{Kalman engine}},\;
\underbrace{W,b_{\text{OU}},\pi_{\text{OU}},c_{\text{OU}}}_{\text{OU engine}})\in\Theta\subset\mathbb R^{10},
\end{equation}
fitted by maximising a backtest objective subject to a minimum-trade-count constraint. The
objective surface is \textbf{piecewise constant}: a fill is a step indicator
$\mathbf 1\{L_t\le B_t(\theta)\}$, so the gradient is zero almost everywhere and jumps at fill
boundaries. Gradient and quasi-Newton methods are therefore useless, and the fit uses
\textbf{differential evolution} --- a population-based, derivative-free global optimiser ---
seeded by a Sobol sequence with the incumbent parameters injected as one member, under a fixed
random seed for reproducibility.

To avoid fragile knife-edge optima, the objective is averaged over a neighbourhood of each
candidate: with multiplicative perturbations $\rho^{(p)}\sim\mathcal U(0.97,1.03)$,
\begin{equation}
\tilde S(\theta)=\tfrac12 S(\theta)+\tfrac12\frac1P\sum_{p=1}^{P}
S\bigl(\Pi_\Theta[\theta\odot\rho^{(p)}]\bigr),
\end{equation}
which penalises spikes and favours plateaus. The same philosophy extends to reporting: planning
figures are quoted at the parameter-neighbourhood median, not at the fitted point.

\section{The validation programme}\label{sec:validation}

The largest single body of work is not either model but the testing regime that decides what gets
deployed. Its main components:

\begin{itemize}[topsep=2pt,itemsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{Walk-forward re-optimisation audits.} Whether parameters should be re-fitted to
      recent data was tested directly, across in-sample, fit-one-year-test-the-next, and rolling
      walk-forward designs. Re-fitted parameters beat the incumbents in 6 of 40 out-of-sample
      folds on the daily model and 1 of 18 on a since-retired weekly variant --- a decisive
      negative that now governs the firm's change-control: parameters are frozen, and only
      specification corrections (not re-fits) are eligible for deployment.
\item \textbf{Allocation research.} Equal weighting across names was tested against
      inverse-volatility, Sharpe-proportional and return-proportional weighting schemes (it won
      by roughly 10pp out of sample); the capital split between the two engines was tested
      across the full range, on return and on risk-adjusted objectives, with the buffer
      re-fitted inside each fold --- the analysis that moved the book from a 75:25 to a flat
      50:50 split.
\item \textbf{Execution verification.} The backtests are not simulations of a formula alone: a
      Python replica of the production engine reproduces the live workbook's cached results
      exactly, and same-day round trips are verified against 1-minute or 5-minute intraday bars.
\item \textbf{Stress replay.} The full book, including its deployed protection gate (a
      breadth-conditional 200-day moving-average rule), was replayed through the 2022 bear
      market on intraday data to measure drawdown behaviour in a regime the fitting history
      does not contain.
\end{itemize}

\section{Deployment and scale}

The system runs live on nine US-listed names, each on both engines with its own fitted
ten-parameter vector, pooled in a single capital loop and operated daily through a
spreadsheet-and-broker production stack with automated data refresh. The modelled activity level
is on the order of five hundred fills per year. The development is documented across a suite of
internal papers --- a full methodology paper, an operations manual, a
book-construction and ranking memorandum, a bear-market protection study, and a strategy
evolution record --- together with the research codebase (optimisers, replay engines, validation
harnesses and operational tooling) from which every quoted figure is reproducible.
